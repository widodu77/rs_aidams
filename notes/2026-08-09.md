## The fixed-policy frontier

> Everything in this section is retained deliberately as the record of what was believed at
> this point. The numbers are real, but they were produced under 4-bit NF4 quantization at 100
> items per category. The fp16 rerun keeps the overall shape and overturns two specific claims.

500 items per policy, 100 per category, Qwen3-1.7B 4-bit NF4, greedy, cap 768.

| category | never acc | always acc | never tok | always tok |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `simple_python` | 88.0% | **95.0%** | 35.1 | 176.9 |
| `multiple` | **93.0%** | 86.0% | 35.5 | 204.7 |
| `parallel` | 18.0% | **67.0%** | 46.4 | 236.5 |
| `parallel_multiple` | 29.0% | **70.0%** | 57.0 | 362.3 |
| `irrelevance` | 31.0% | **81.0%** | 27.1 | 254.2 |
| **OVERALL** | 51.8% | **79.8%** | 40.2 | 246.9 |

Reasoning buys +28.0 points overall for 6.1× the tokens.

**Neither policy dominates, and the exception survived the full sample.** On `multiple` — one call, several candidate functions — not thinking is better, 93.0% against 86.0%. This was visible at n=41 (93.0 vs 82.9) and held at n=100 (93.0 vs 86.0); the gap narrowed but did not close or reverse. That single row is the empirical case for the project: a fixed policy is leaving accuracy on the table in both directions, so a per-call gate has something real to capture. It is not an artefact of the small partial run.

The largest gains are where reasoning has actual work to do — decomposing a request into several calls (`parallel` +49, `parallel_multiple` +41) and deciding that no function applies (`irrelevance` +50). `never` scores 31% on irrelevance: without reasoning the model simply calls something.

Diagnostics are clean. Truncation ≤5% everywhere (worst on `always/irrelevance`), so the cap is not driving the accuracy numbers. `never` think-rate is exactly 0.0% across all 500 items, so that policy is perfectly enforced.

## Finding: the base model already gates, and gates badly on hard items

> **⚠ SUPERSEDED — this finding did not survive. It was an artefact of 4-bit quantization.**
> Retained in full because the reasoning below is sound given the data available at the time,
> and because how it was overturned is itself the most useful thing recorded today. Of the 77
> self-gated items identified here, fp16 self-gates exactly **1**. See the correction section.

`always` think-rate is **not** 1.0 — it is 84.6%, and it varies sharply by category (`multiple` 97%, `irrelevance` 95%, `parallel_multiple` 85%, `simple_python` 82%, `parallel` 64%). The cause is not a prompting failure: with `enable_thinking=True` Qwen3 opens `<think>` and immediately closes it, emitting `<think>\n</think>` and going straight to the call. It declines to reason.

Splitting the `always` run by whether the model actually reasoned:

| category | self-gated n | gated acc | reasoned n | reasoned acc |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `simple_python` | 18 | 94.4% | 82 | 95.1% |
| `multiple` | 3 | 66.7% | 97 | 86.6% |
| `parallel` | 36 | 47.2% | 64 | 78.1% |
| `parallel_multiple` | 15 | 46.7% | 85 | 74.1% |
| `irrelevance` | 5 | 80.0% | 95 | 81.1% |
| **all** | 77 | 61.0% | 423 | 83.2% |

On `simple_python` the gate is well calibrated — gated items score 94.4% against 95.1% for reasoned ones, i.e. it skipped reasoning exactly where reasoning was not buying anything. On those 18 items, 13 produced a call byte-identical to the `never` run's.

On `parallel` and `parallel_multiple` it is not: gated items score ~47% against ~78%/74% for reasoned ones. **The base model's gate fires hardest on the category where reasoning helps most** (`parallel`, +49 points), skipping it on more than a third of items.

**This is an observation, not yet a causal claim, and the confound must stay visible.** The model chooses which items to gate, so gated items are a self-selected set and may simply be harder. What the table shows is that the gate is *not* selecting easy items on the parallel categories, which is what a well-calibrated gate would do — and demonstrably does do on `simple_python`. It does not show what those 36 items would have scored had the model been forced to reason.

The counterfactual is cheap to obtain (77 items) and would convert this into a result: force reasoning on exactly the gated items and see whether accuracy recovers toward the reasoned-item rate. Noted as the next experiment.

Two consequences either way:

1. **The `always` anchor is not a true upper bound.** It is "always, except on the 15.4% the model declines", and the exclusions are concentrated in the parallel categories. Every reported `always` number carries this caveat and the writeup must state it rather than presenting a clean fixed-policy baseline.
2. **It raises the bar the learned gate has to clear.** The comparison is no longer "learned gate vs no gate" but "learned gate vs the crude one Qwen3 already has". That is a harder and more honest test, and the miscalibration on `parallel` is where the headroom is.

## Phase C — reward function

`src/rewards/reward.py`, with `tests/test_reward.py`. Suite now 49 tests (29 scorer, 20 reward), all passing.

```
reward = w_correct * correct + w_format * format_ok - lambda_think * min(think_tokens / budget, 1)
```

Three decisions that are not obvious and are worth defending:

**Only think tokens are priced, never completion length.** Penalising total length would charge the model for the tool call itself, and call length is a property of the category — `parallel_multiple` needs several call blocks by construction. Pricing completion length would convert a category effect into an apparent reasoning effect, exactly the confound this project has to avoid. Pinned by a test.

**The penalty is normalised by a token budget, not charged per raw token.** With raw counts `lambda_think` has to be ~1e-3 to be commensurate with a correctness of 1.0, and its meaning changes silently whenever the generation cap changes. Normalising makes the cost bounded in `[0, lambda_think]`, so lambda reads directly as "the most thinking can ever cost, in units of one correct call" — which is the quantity the Phase E curve is parameterised by. Clipped at the budget so an over-long generation, which already scores zero correctness for never reaching the call, is not punished twice.

**`w_format = 0.2`, not 1.0.** This one nearly became a bug. `format_ok` means "nothing malformed was emitted" and is therefore True for prose containing no tool call at all — deliberately, from the Phase A split. At a weight near correctness that makes silence competitive: the model collects most of the reward by never risking a call. At 0.2 it stays a tie-breaker between two genuine attempts. Pinned by a test asserting that well-formed prose scores below a correct call.

An empty `<think></think>` is charged zero and does not count as reasoning, following directly from the self-gating finding — the empty block is the model declining to reason, so charging it would price a decision it did not make.

The reward is deliberately not category-aware beyond what `score_sample` already does. No hand-written rule about when reasoning is appropriate may enter the reward, because that is the thing being learned. The reward states what thinking costs; whether it was worth paying is settled by whether correctness rose.

`make_reward_fn` adapts this to TRL's `fn(prompts, completions, **columns) -> list[float]` signature, tested explicitly — a shape mismatch there surfaces as an opaque trainer error many minutes into a GPU run. `compute_reward` returns every component separately, because a policy that collapsed to never-think and one that started emitting malformed calls both flatten the reward curve and only the component traces distinguish them.

## Moving generation to Colab + vLLM

The counterfactual and the full-category reruns were both blocked on the same thing: local
generation was too slow to run at scale. Measured why, rather than assuming it was the card.

On the 359-item local run, **14229 decode steps produced 96609 useful tokens — a 2.36x waste
factor.** HF `generate` batches statically: every sequence steps until the *longest* in its
batch finishes, so one rambling item drags fifteen idle slots with it. Length-sorting the
same batches would have cut 52% of the steps, but completion length isn't knowable in advance.
That is exactly what vLLM's continuous batching solves — a finished slot is refilled
immediately. Projected local cost for the remaining work was ~16 h.

Three obstacles, each worth recording:

1. **`bfcl-eval` vs vLLM over torch.** A full install fights vLLM's torch pin. Resolved by
   noticing that `bfcl_eval/__init__.py` is empty: `import bfcl_eval` costs 0.002s and 38
   modules, while importing `ast_checker` costs 9.8s and 4322 modules (dragging `qwen_agent`
   and `soundfile`). Deferring that one import into `score_sample` makes the module importable
   under `pip install --no-deps bfcl-eval`, since generation needs only the *data*. This is the
   mitigation for the Colab fragility risk flagged back in Phase A — it turned out to be a
   one-line change.
2. **CUDA ABI mismatch.** vLLM 0.26.0 ships a CUDA 13 binary; Colab preinstalls torch
   `2.11.0+cu128`. pip saw the version `2.11.0` satisfied and left torch alone, so a cu13 `.so`
   went hunting for `libcudart.so.13` on a cu12 system. Fixed by reinstalling the same torch
   *version* from the cu130 index. The lesson: a matching version number does not imply a
   matching CUDA ABI.
3. **T4 is sm_75**, exactly at CUDA 13's minimum. No bf16 (vLLM casts to fp16), no
   FlashAttention (falls back to TRITON_ATTN). Both are warnings, not errors. A residual
   `libnvrtc.so.13` error appears in vLLM's *shutdown* path only, after records are written.

**Result: 3 policies x 1240 items in 36 minutes**, against the ~16 h local projection. Capping
`max_model_len` at 4096 (vs Qwen3's native 32k) bought 21.6x concurrency out of 9.46 GiB of KV
cache; the worst observed prompt is 905 tokens, so the cap has 2.4x headroom.

## Correction: 4-bit quantization was distorting the result

The local runs used `--load-4bit` because the 3050 has 4 GB. Colab has 15 GB, so vLLM ran fp16
unquantized. Comparing the two on the **same 500 items** shows the quantization was not merely
costing accuracy — it was changing conclusions.

**1. The self-gating finding is dead.**

| category | NF4 self-gated | fp16 self-gated |
|---|---|---|
| `simple_python` | 18/100 | 0/100 |
| `parallel` | 36/100 | 2/100 |
| all | 77/500 | 11/500 |

Of the 77 items NF4 self-gated, fp16 gates **one**. Not a shifted distribution — the phenomenon
essentially does not exist at full precision. Empty `<think></think>` blocks were NF4
degradation, not a property of Qwen3-1.7B.

**2. The `multiple` inversion is dead, and it reversed.** On the identical 100 items: NF4 gave
never 93.0 / always 86.0; fp16 gives never 92.0 / **always 94.0**. At the full n=200, always
wins 95.0 to 93.5 (McNemar p=0.58).

**3. Why.** Quantization was crippling the `never` policy specifically. On shared items `never`
went 51.8% -> 71.0%, driven by `parallel` at 18% -> 67%. Without reasoning to lean on, the
quantized model could not emit several correct calls; reasoning was masking quantization damage.
So every "thinking helps" gap was inflated, and one category flipped sign outright.

**This is the methodological result of the day, and it belongs in the paper:** on a 1.7B model,
NF4 did not just cost accuracy — it flipped the sign of the reasoning effect on two categories.
Anyone running small-model reasoning experiments on a consumer GPU is exposed to this.

Also retired: the planned counterfactual on the 77 self-gated items. There is nothing left to
measure.

## The result that replaces them: prompting cannot produce a gate

The `adaptive` prompt — reasoning explicitly optional, both output shapes shown, neither
privileged — had never been run. It reasons on **96.9%** of items and spends **287.5** mean
tokens, *more* than `always` at 266.9, for statistically indistinguishable accuracy
(87.5% vs 87.3%).

This is the control the whole project needs. The strongest rival hypothesis to "RL learned a
gate" was always "you did not need RL, you could just ask". That hypothesis is now dead, and it
died before any training was run.

## The per-item oracle: the target

Both policies ran on the same 1240 items, so every item is paired.

| | accuracy | mean tokens |
|---|---|---|
| never | 73.8% | 51.9 |
| always | 87.3% | 266.9 |
| adaptive | 87.5% | 287.5 |
| **oracle** | **91.2%** | **96.7** |

- thinking **rescues** a failure on 216 items (17.4%)
- thinking **destroys** a success on 49 items (4.0%)
- neither policy is right on 109 items (8.8%) — the model's irreducible floor, so 91.2% is the ceiling

The oracle peeks at ground truth, so it is a bound and not a target. But it reframes what
success looks like: **+3.9 accuracy points over always-think at 2.8x fewer tokens.** The prize
is the cost axis. A paper claiming adaptive beats always on accuracy has 3.9 points to fight
noise over; one claiming it matches always-think accuracy at a third of the cost has a
170-token gap to work with.

**The oracle thinks on 17.4% of items.** That is the number to watch during training — a policy
landing near 17-20% is in the right regime, near 0% or near 100% is collapse. Far more
informative than a reward curve.

## Where the lambda sweep has to sit

Recomputed from the fp16 baselines (the earlier NF4-derived range was wrong):

| category | delta accuracy | lambda* |
|---|---|---|
| `simple_python` | **-2.8%** | never worth thinking |
| `multiple` | +1.5% | 0.07 |
| `parallel` | +15.5% | 0.48 |
| `parallel_multiple` | +19.0% | 0.53 |
| `irrelevance` | +44.2% | 2.11 |

Lopsided: four of five categories switch off below 0.55, then nothing changes until
`irrelevance` at 2.11. A sweep over {0.05, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0} covers every transition;
a linear sweep to 3.0 would waste half its points in dead space.

It also yields a falsifiable prediction for the trained policy: **think on irrelevance, think
on the parallel categories, skip simple ones.** That is testable independently of the Pareto
curve, and it connects directly to H1.
