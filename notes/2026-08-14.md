# 2026-08-14 — Phase E result: the gate does not form, and pairing backfired on length

Ten adapters evaluated on the 248 held-out items with the adaptive prompt, greedy decoding, fp16,
against the three fixed-policy anchors and the per-item oracle. Two sweeps: the original
free-sampling one and the paired-rollout one, trained on identical data with identical seeds,
differing only in whether each GRPO group was forced to contain both thinking modes.

## 1. No gate, at any λ, under either rollout scheme

Think rate is 98–99% everywhere, and per-category it is 95–100% in every trained run. The
prediction from the fp16 break-even table — that reasoning would switch off in order,
`simple_python` first and `irrelevance` last — does not appear at all. `simple_python`, the
category where thinking helps least, is at 100% in nine of ten adapters.

**Pairing fixed exploration during training and none of it transferred.** Train-time think rate was
pinned at 0.500 by construction for all 100 steps of every paired run; eval-time think rate is
indistinguishable from the untrained adaptive prompt. The comparison "reason vs do not" was made
tens of thousands of times and left no trace in the deployed policy.

The reason is the structural asymmetry flagged before the sweep ran and now confirmed: a direct
rollout is generated with `<think></think>` already prefilled *into the prompt*, so its completion
never contains the decision not to think. Reinforcing it raises probability under a prompt that is
never deployed. Only the negative direction can transfer — thinking rollouts that lose get negative
advantage, lowering P(`<think>`) under the eval prompt — and that channel was evidently far too
weak, which is consistent with λ reaching only 31.2% of steps (2026-08-13).

## 2. λ still does nothing, now measured at the policy level

Accuracy across the paired sweep runs 87.9–90.7% and tokens 259.5–271.8, neither monotone in λ over
a 40× range. The two best accuracies (90.7% at λ=0.5 and λ=1.0) sit 1.6 points above the adaptive
prompt, which on n=248 is four items — well inside noise, and not to be reported as an improvement.

This is exactly what the training-side analysis predicted. λ cancels out of the normalised advantage
on 68.8% of steps and reaches the gradient only through a 1e-4 stabiliser there; a flat policy-level
response is the expected consequence, not a surprise.

## 3. The unexpected result: pairing made reasoning *longer*

The paired adapters reason 189–198 tokens; the unpaired ones 151–168; the untrained adaptive prompt
196. **Free sampling shortened reasoning by ~30–45 tokens. Pairing left it unchanged.** Tested per
item, same items, same λ, only the rollout scheme differing (Wilcoxon signed-rank, two-sided):

| λ | paired | unpaired | Δ mean | Δ median | z | p |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.05 | 193.6 | 168.4 | +25.2 | +10.0 | 6.31 | 2.8e-10 |
| 0.25 | 198.1 | 166.0 | +32.2 | +20.0 | 7.76 | 8.2e-15 |
| 0.5 | 188.6 | 150.8 | +37.8 | +20.0 | 9.20 | 3.5e-20 |
| 1.0 | 194.9 | 160.3 | +34.6 | +23.5 | 7.70 | 1.4e-14 |
| 2.0 | 191.1 | 165.9 | +25.1 | +15.0 | 7.81 | 5.6e-15 |

Consistent in sign at every λ and overwhelming in significance. The intervention introduced to
*reduce* reasoning cost increased it relative to the control.

**The mechanism follows from where the length variance lives.** Under free sampling all eight
rollouts reason, so within-group length variance is variance *among thinking rollouts*, and
"shorter wins" acts directly on think length — under the prompt that is actually deployed. Under
pairing the shortest rollouts in every group are the direct ones with think=0, and they absorb the
positive advantage; but they were generated under the prefilled no-think prompt, so that
reinforcement lands somewhere the eval never visits. The four thinking rollouts meanwhile receive
similar negative advantage regardless of *their own* lengths, which removes most of the
within-thinking length pressure that free sampling had.

So pairing traded a real length signal on the deployed prompt for a larger-looking one on an
undeployed prompt. That is a mechanistic account, consistent with every number above, and it should
be labelled as an interpretation rather than a measurement — the measurement is the table.

## 4. What this says about the research question

The project asked whether a ≤2B model can learn to decide, per call, whether to reason first. Three
findings now constrain the answer, and none of them is "the model cannot":

1. **The capability gap is real and large.** The per-item oracle reaches 92.7% at 99.5 tokens
   against always-think's 89.5% at 264.1 — better accuracy at 38% of the cost. A gate is worth
   having; nothing here says the model could not represent one.
2. **Prompting cannot produce it.** The adaptive prompt thinks 96.8% of the time.
3. **This GRPO objective cannot train it,** for a reason that is now derived rather than observed:
   group-standardised advantages divide out any reward term that is the sole source of within-group
   variance, so a length penalty is inert on every group where correctness and format are constant
   — 68.8% of steps here.

That third point is the contribution. It is a statement about the *optimiser–reward interaction*,
not about the model, and it predicts the failure of any `correct − λ·length` objective under vanilla
GRPO, not just this one.

## Next

- **The one experiment that would test the diagnosis directly:** two groups per generation batch
  (`per_device 4, accum 4`) with `scale_rewards="batch"`. The normaliser is then computed over a
  quantity λ does *not* solely determine, so λ should survive. If think rate moves, the diagnosis is
  confirmed and the frontier becomes reportable; if it does not, the null hardens into a statement
  about the model. One run, ~20 minutes.
- A second candidate, cheaper to argue in the paper: make the direct rollouts generate the empty
  `<think></think>` *as completion* rather than receiving it prefilled, so the decision not to think
  is inside the gradient. This is the structural fix for §1 and is independent of the λ issue.
